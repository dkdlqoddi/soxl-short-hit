Stock market today: Dow plunges 1,800 points, Nasdaq enters correction in AI trade meltdown

US stocks plunged on Thursday as AI and chip stocks sold off, erasing the gains from Wednesday's post-election rally.

The Dow Jones Industrial Average (^DJI) tumbled 3.4%, or more than 1,800 points. The S&P 500 (^GSPC) fell 4.3%, and the Nasdaq Composite (^IXIC) dropped 5.5%, with the tech-heavy index closing in correction territory.

The Nasdaq-100 (^NDX) fell 6.3%, erasing $2.3 trillion in market value.

The PHLX Semiconductor index (^SOX) plummeted 12% as Nvidia (NVDA), Micron (MU), and other chip names sank. Wall Street unloaded this year's AI winners, while large-cap tech stocks saw the worst day since the COVID-19 pandemic sell-off.

Investors continued to process the Democrats' sweeping victory on Tuesday and its implications for markets and the economy. Amid relief that election uncertainty has been removed, the stock market continues to sort out the perceived winners and losers of a regime change on Capitol Hill.

Oil prices rose amid uncertainty that President Trump would pursue a deal with Iran to end the war in the Middle East. Optimism grew overnight after Vice President JD Vance said, "The reason we lost [the midterm elections] is the war," in a post on X suggesting the party should push for a resolution to the conflict. Brent crude oil (BZ=F) prices traded around $98 per barrel, while US benchmark WTI crude (CL=F) stood at $90 per barrel.

    Retail traders dump $358 million in stocks in biggest selling day since 2020

    Retail investors are dumping US stocks at a pace not seen since the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, breaking with their typical market-down-day habit of "buying the dip."

    Everyday investors sold a net $358 million in individual US stocks on Thursday as of 2:58 p.m. ET, according to Vanda Research, putting them on track for their biggest single-day net selling spree since March 17, 2020, when retail investors pulled $502 million from individual stocks.

    "This is what we would call a total capitulation by retail investors," Viraj Patel, global macro strategist at Vanda Research, said in emailed comments.

    The selling marks a departure from retail investors' tendency to scoop up shares when markets tumble. Vanda said there were "no signs of buying the dip" in individual stocks, though some investors continued purchasing leveraged exchange-traded funds such as the ProShares UltraPro QQQ (TQQQ) and Direxion Daily Semiconductor Bull 3X Shares (SOXL).

    Patel added that the retreat echoed a broader "buyers' strike" among institutional investors alongside forced selling by systematic and leveraged investment strategies.

    Goldman Sachs adds record number of new partners

    Goldman Sachs (GS) promoted 109 employees to its coveted partnership on Thursday, its largest class since 2010, as the Wall Street giant elevated record numbers of asset managers and engineers to its senior ranks.

    The new class is nearly 15% larger than in 2024 when Goldman named 95 partners, according to a statement from the bank.

    Of the 109 new partners, 60 work in Goldman's investment banking and trading division, while 33 are in Asset & Wealth Management. The firm also promoted 13 engineers.

    In a departure from previous years, a Goldman executive declined to disclose the gender and racial makeup of the new class, citing changes in the legal environment. The executive added that with the latest round of promotions, the firm has more than 100 female partners.

    Partnership status at Goldman is considered highly exclusive. The firm promotes new partners every two years. The promotion comes with higher compensation and additional financial benefits, including opportunities to invest in the firm's private funds.

    Once promotions take effect in January, Goldman will have a little over 500 partners, an executive said, amounting to roughly 1% of Goldman's workforce.

    What is a correction?

    It's a bad day for the stock market.

    Major indexes are falling Thursday, with technology stocks leading the sell-off and semiconductor shares among the biggest decliners.

    But one rough session doesn't necessarily amount to what investors deem a correction.

    A correction generally refers to a decline of at least 10% from a recent high in a stock, index, or other asset. It is typically seen as a meaningful pullback, but one that stops short of a bear market, which is commonly defined as a drop of 20% or more.

    That distinction matters after Wednesday's sharp rally. The tech-heavy Nasdaq Composite (^IXIC) and the narrower Nasdaq-100 are each on pace to close Thursday in correction territory, as is the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index (^SOX). 

    Meanwhile, the Dow and S&P 500 (down 3% and 4% from yesterday) aren't close to correction territory.

    Chip stocks get slaughtered as Wall Street heads for its worst post-midterm day since 1896

    Wall Street's midterm celebration lasted one day. Chip stocks are getting taken to the woodshed Thursday, and the Dow Jones Industrial Average (^DJI) is heading for its worst second session after a midterm election in data going back to its start in 1896.

    It took the Nasdaq Composite (^IXIC) six minutes to erase its entire post-election rally. Then the selling got worse.

    This week has an echo from 1896. The day after William McKinley won the White House that November, the Dow jumped about 4.5%, then gave back about 2% the next session. On Wednesday, the Dow jumped 3%, its best day after a midterm since 1982.

    Bitcoin dives as post-election stock rally fades

    Bitcoin (BTC-USD) briefly tumbled below $76,000 on Thursday before stabilizing above the threshold as a broad sell-off in US stocks threatened to erase the previous day's post-election rally.

    The world's largest cryptocurrency fell more than 5% Thursday, reversing a sharp rebound Wednesday that had lifted bitcoin above $80,000 as investors cheered midterm election results showing Democrats swept both chambers of Congress. The move has followed major indexes shedding their Wednesday gains.

    Bitcoin's retreat also underscored of the crypto market's lingering fragility following $6.3 billion hack of Binance, the world's largest cryptocurrency exchange, which rattled crypto prices last week.

    The Democratic takeover of Congress also has raised fresh questions the crypto industry's prospects in Washington, including passage of the industry's long-stalled bill, the Clarity Act.

    Brian Gardner, Stifel's chief Washington policy strategist, said in a briefing with reporters on Thursday that the Clarity Act is unlikely to advance before the end of the year.

    "It's off the table for the lame duck session," Gardner said, adding that the odds of a bipartisan compromise in the next Congress are "pretty low."

    Gardner added that he expects Senator Elizabeth Warren, who is poised to chair the Senate Banking Committee, to pursue investigations targeting crypto firms along with Wall Street banks and private equity firms.
